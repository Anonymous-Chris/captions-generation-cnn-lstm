Metric	Main idea	Needs human references?	Higher is better?
BLEU-1	Matching individual words	Yes	Yes
BLEU-2	Matching 2-word phrases	Yes	Yes
BLEU-3	Matching 3-word phrases	Yes	Yes
BLEU-4	Matching 4-word phrases	Yes	Yes
METEOR	Word overlap plus more flexible linguistic matching	Yes	Yes
ROUGE-L	Longest matching word sequence	Yes	Yes
CIDEr	How well a caption agrees with the consensus of human captions	Yes	Yes
CLIPScore	How well the caption semantically matches the image itself	No	Yes

BLEU

BLEU compares your generated caption with human-written reference captions using n-grams.

Suppose the generated caption is:

a man rides a bicycle

and a human reference is:

a man rides a bicycle on the street

BLEU-1 checks individual words:

a
man
rides
a
bicycle

So it mainly asks:

Did the generated caption use the same words as the references?

BLEU-1 is relatively forgiving.

BLEU-2 checks pairs of consecutive words:

a man
man rides
rides a
a bicycle

Now word order matters more.

BLEU-3 checks groups of three:

a man rides
man rides a
rides a bicycle

BLEU-4 checks groups of four:

a man rides a
man rides a bicycle

BLEU-4 is therefore much stricter.

For example:

Reference:
a man rides a bicycle
Generated:
a person is riding a bike

Semantically, these are very similar.

But BLEU-4 may be low because there is almost no exact 4-word overlap.

That is one reason your BLEU-4 correlation with CLIPScore was only:

0.386

while BLEU-1 was:

0.510

As BLEU becomes more dependent on exact longer phrases, it can diverge more from semantic similarity.

⸻

METEOR

METEOR is more flexible than BLEU.

It considers things like:

* exact word matches
* word stems
* some synonym relationships
* word ordering

For example:

Reference:
a man is running
Generated:
a person runs

BLEU might penalize this fairly heavily.

METEOR can recognize that:

running
runs

are closely related.

So METEOR is trying to capture meaning somewhat better than plain n-gram overlap.

Your CLIPScore/METEOR correlation was:

0.495

which is moderate.

⸻

ROUGE-L

ROUGE-L looks for the Longest Common Subsequence.

That means it asks:

What is the longest sequence of words appearing in both captions in the same order?

The words don’t necessarily have to be directly next to each other.

Example:

Reference:
a young man is riding a bicycle down the street
Generated:
a man is riding a bicycle

A long matching sequence exists:

a man is riding a bicycle

So ROUGE-L would probably be fairly high.

This makes ROUGE-L less strict than BLEU-4 about exact consecutive 4-word structures.

In your results, ROUGE-L had the strongest correlation with CLIPScore among the reference-based metrics:

ROUGE-L ↔ CLIPScore = 0.540

Still only moderate, though.

⸻

CIDEr

CIDEr was created specifically for image caption evaluation.

It compares the generated caption against multiple human captions and asks, roughly:

Does this generated caption describe the same important things that humans consistently describe?

It uses n-grams too, but weights them using something similar to TF-IDF.

Common generic words are less important.

For example:

a
the
is

don’t contribute as much.

More informative phrases like:

playing guitar
red motorcycle
snow covered mountain

matter more.

This is useful with Flickr8k because you have approximately five human captions per image.

Suppose four references mention:

dog

and only one says something unrelated to the dog.

A generated caption mentioning the dog is likely to score well because that concept is part of the human-caption consensus.

Your correlation was:

CIDEr ↔ CLIPScore = 0.536

That’s one of the higher CLIPScore correlations in your experiment.

But CIDEr still depends on the human references.

⸻

CLIPScore

CLIPScore is fundamentally different from all the metrics above.

Instead of asking:

Does this generated caption resemble the human captions?

it asks:

Does this generated caption semantically match the actual image?

CLIP has learned representations for both:

IMAGE
   ↓
image embedding
TEXT
   ↓
text embedding

Then it measures how similar those representations are.

So imagine the image contains a child riding a bicycle.

Human reference:

a young boy riding his bicycle outside

Generated caption:

a child cycling outdoors

BLEU-4 might be poor because the wording is very different.

But CLIPScore could be high because:

child ≈ young boy
cycling ≈ riding bicycle
outdoors ≈ outside

and more importantly, those concepts fit the actual image.

That’s why CLIPScore is useful for your research.

⸻

The critical distinction

Most of your metrics evaluate:

Generated Caption
        ↕
Human References

Specifically:

BLEU
METEOR
ROUGE-L
CIDEr

CLIPScore evaluates:

Generated Caption
        ↕
Actual Image

That difference is central to your project.

A simple example

Imagine an image of:

A brown dog jumping into a swimming pool.

Human references might say:

a dog jumps into the water
a brown dog leaps into a pool
a dog jumping toward a swimming pool
a dog is jumping into the water
a brown animal jumping into a pool

Generated caption A:

a brown dog leaps into a swimming pool

Almost every metric should be high.

Now generated caption B:

a dog jumps into the water

BLEU/CIDEr probably still do well, and CLIPScore should also be good.

Generated caption C:

a canine dives into a pool

Semantically excellent.

But BLEU-4 might be low because the exact wording differs substantially.

CLIPScore might remain high.

Now generated caption D:

a brown dog jumps over a man

It may accidentally share words like:

brown dog jumps

with the human references.

A lexical metric could still give it some credit.

But CLIPScore may penalize it because there is no man in the image and the action is wrong.

That type of case is what our disagreement analysis is trying to find.

How to think about them

You can mentally group your metrics into three families:

Lexical / n-gram
├── BLEU-1
├── BLEU-2
├── BLEU-3
└── BLEU-4
Reference similarity
├── METEOR
├── ROUGE-L
└── CIDEr
Image-semantic
└── CLIPScore

They’re not perfectly separate categories—CIDEr also uses n-grams, for example—but this is a useful conceptual way to understand your experiment.
